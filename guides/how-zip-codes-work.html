ZIP basics

How ZIP codes actually work

The ZIP Code — short for Zone Improvement Plan — was introduced by the US Post Office Department on 1 July 1963. Postal inspector Robert A. Moon had proposed the idea of a coded sorting system back in 1944. Within three months of launch, roughly 30% of mail already carried a ZIP.

What each digit means

  • Digit 1 — the national area. 0 in the Northeast through 9 on the West Coast. A ZIP starting with 3 is in AL, FL, GA, MS or TN.
  • Digits 2–3 — the sectional center facility (SCF). The mail-processing hub. 322xx is the Jacksonville, Florida area. Try the prefix decoder.
  • Digits 4–5 — the delivery area — a post office or delivery zone within that SCF.

The ZIP+4 extension, added in 1983, narrows delivery to a block face, building, floor or even a single high-volume address.

ZIP codes are routes, not boundaries

ZIPs were designed for delivering mail efficiently, not for drawing maps. A ZIP can cross city or even state lines — over a hundred do. That is why the Census Bureau builds approximate polygons called ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs) for statistics, and why insurers, tax tools and ad platforms often disagree slightly about where a ZIP “is”.

Fun facts

  • Lowest ZIP: 00501 — an IRS center in Holtsville, New York.
  • Highest ZIP: 99950 — Ketchikan, Alaska.
  • Some numbers are never used — like our namesake, 32262.
